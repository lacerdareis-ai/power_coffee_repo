### Body

## The Most Overlooked Ingredient in Cognitive Performance

Ginkgo biloba doesn't get the headlines that caffeine or lion's mane do. It rarely shows up in influencer stacks. It doesn't have a dramatic origin story in Silicon Valley productivity circles.

But it's been studied for longer than most nootropics have existed — and the research on cognitive performance is some of the most consistent in botanical science.

## What Ginkgo Actually Does

The active compounds in ginkgo biloba are flavonoids and terpenoids. Together, they do two things relevant to focus and performance:

First, they act as antioxidants, reducing oxidative stress in brain tissue. Second — and more important for daily performance — they dilate blood vessels and increase cerebral blood flow.

More blood flow to the brain means more oxygen delivery. More oxygen means better signal transmission between neurons. The result isn't a spike. It's sustained cognitive availability — the ability to stay sharp through a long morning, a demanding meeting, or a project that needs real depth.

## What the Studies Show

Research using 120mg of standardized ginkgo extract daily shows measurable improvements in:

- **Attention and concentration** — particularly under stress or fatigue
- **Working memory** — the ability to hold and process information in real time
- **Processing speed** — how quickly the brain retrieves and applies information

These aren't self-reported outcomes. Several studies use neuroimaging to show actual changes in brain activity patterns — not just how subjects feel, but how their brains are functioning.

## Why It Belongs Alongside Caffeine

Caffeine is a stimulant. It activates the central nervous system and temporarily blocks adenosine — the compound that makes you feel tired. It's effective. It's fast. And it's finite.

Ginkgo biloba operates differently. It supports the underlying system caffeine runs on. When blood flow is optimized and oxidative stress is lower, caffeine works better and the energy lasts longer. That's not a marketing claim — it's basic physiology.

function addRect(parent, x, y, w, h, color, opacity = 1) {
  const r = figma.createRectangle();
  r.x = x; r.y = y; r.resize(w, h);
  r.fills = [{ type: "SOLID", color: col(color), opacity }];
  parent.appendChild(r);
  return r;
}

async function addText(parent, content, x, y, w, size, weight, color, align = "LEFT", lineH = 1.05) {
  await figma.loadFontAsync({ family: "Inter", style: weight });
  const t = figma.createText();
  t.fontName = { family: "Inter", style: weight };
  t.characters = content;
  t.fontSize = size;
  t.textAlignHorizontal = align;
  t.fills = [{ type: "SOLID", color: col(color) }];
  t.lineHeight = { unit: "PERCENT", value: lineH * 100 };
  t.x = x; t.y = y;
  t.resize(w, t.height);
  parent.appendChild(t);
  return t;
}

async function buildSlide(data, offsetX) {
  const frame = figma.createFrame();
  frame.name = `Slide ${data.id}  -  ${data.type}`;
  frame.resize(W, H);

  frame.fills = [{ type: "SOLID", color: col(data.bg) }];
  frame.clipsContent = true;
