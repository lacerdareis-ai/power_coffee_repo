# I paid to have a stranger fact-check my own product. It found three things I'd been getting wrong.

*Why a new brand needs someone else to vouch for it — and what certification actually costs you beyond the fee.*

---

Nobody has a reason to believe a new brand.

That is not pessimism, it is just the maths. I make a functional coffee blend. So do a few hundred other people. A buyer looking at my bag has no history with me, no friend who drinks it, no shelf presence to reassure them. Everything they know about the product comes from a company whose entire incentive is to make it sound good. Mine.

So when I say "clean energy, no crash," I am asking a stranger to take the word of the person selling it. If I were them, I would not either.

In July, The Power Coffee was certified by Illuminate Labs — third-party, medically reviewed, signed on 29 July. That sentence is the least interesting part of this post. What happened during the process is what I actually want to talk about, because it was not what I expected.

## What certification actually is

I think most founders imagine certification as a badge you buy. You send a fee, you get a seal, you put the seal on the bag, conversion goes up.

That is not what it is. What you are buying is an outside party going through your claims and your formula and deciding, on the record, which of your statements they are willing to attach their name to. Their name has value precisely because they will refuse.

In our case that meant looking at the actives and checking them against published research. The taurine claim held up against human endurance data — the literature is fairly direct that endurance performance can improve from a single oral dose in the 1–6 gram range, and our certified panel is 2.1 g. The caffeine safety question held up against the European Food Safety Authority's position on single doses. No ingredients on the banned list. A physician reviewed it for medical accuracy.

None of that was written by me. That is the entire point. It is the first statement about my product that does not come from me.

## The part I did not expect

Here is what nobody tells you: the certification does not just validate your product. It audits your marketing.

When the certified nutrition panel came back, I put it next to what we had been publishing. Three things did not line up.

We had been citing a specific protein figure in content. The certified panel does not state one — pea protein is in the formula, first on the ingredient list, but no gram amount was verified. So we had been repeating a number that was not on any verified document. Not a lie, but not something I could point at either.

We had been saying taurine in round numbers. The verified figure is 2.1 grams. A trivial difference that nobody would catch, which is exactly why it matters — if I am rounding where it flatters me, why would anyone trust the numbers where it counts?

And the ingredient list threw up a mismatch I am now having corrected — proof that the checking runs in both directions, and that a document is only useful if you actually read it against the thing it describes.

Every one of those came from us writing marketing faster than we were verifying it. Nobody was being dishonest. We were being loose. Certification is what made loose visible, because suddenly there was an external document to check ourselves against, and a gap is a gap.

We have stopped citing the protein number. It goes back in when it is on a verified panel and not before.

## Why this matters more for a new brand than an old one

An established brand has other trust mechanisms. Years on shelf. Reviews in the thousands. People who have simply been drinking it since college. They can afford a vague claim because the claim is not what is carrying them.

A new brand has none of that. All you have is what you say and whether anyone believes it. Which means your claims are load-bearing in a way they will never be again — and a load-bearing claim you cannot substantiate is the most fragile thing in the business. One skeptical buyer, one competitor, one regulator, and it goes.

So the certification does two jobs, and the second is worth more than the first.

The obvious job: a buyer who does not know me can see that somebody who is not me looked at this and signed it. Borrowed credibility, externally verifiable, which is the only kind worth anything at this stage.

The less obvious job: it puts a hard floor under what I am allowed to say. I now have a document, and the document does not care what would convert better this week. Every claim gets checked against it. That constraint is not a cost of certification — for a brand with no track record, it is most of the value.

## What I would tell another founder

Get it earlier than feels comfortable, and for the reason that sounds least exciting.

Do not do it for the badge. The badge is a small conversion lift on a page most people never reach. Do it because it forces you to find out which of your claims are real while you are still small enough that fixing them is a five-minute edit rather than a recall, a lawsuit, or a thread.

I found three loose claims. I would rather have found them in July, at the size I am now, than at ten thousand orders.

One last thing, on how we use it. The seal is on our stories and our packaging, but it never leads. The first line is always the customer's actual problem — the afternoon that gets away from them, the third coffee that stopped working. The certification sits underneath as the reason to believe the rest. Nobody buys a supplement because it was certified. They buy it because they believe it will do something, and the certification is why they believe you.

Trust is not a marketing asset. It is the product's first ingredient.
