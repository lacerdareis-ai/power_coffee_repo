# I spent years selling to businesses. Then I built a brand that sells to people. I copy-pasted my playbook. It almost sank me.


*B2B and B2C aren't the same game played at different speeds. They're different games. Most founders find that out the expensive way.*

---

Before The Power Coffee, I spent years on the B2B side. Long sales cycles. Multiple stakeholders in every deal. A buyer who needed a case study, a reference call, and three internal approvals before they'd say yes to anything.

I got good at that game. Patient. Methodical. Comfortable making a case over months, not moments.

Then I started a coffee brand that sells directly to individual people scrolling Instagram at 9pm, and I brought that entire playbook with me.

It did not work. Not slowly. Immediately.

## What I assumed would transfer

I assumed selling was selling. Find the pain, present the proof, close the deal. The mechanics felt universal.

So I built our first content the way I'd built a deck for a business buyer: credentials up front, ingredient specs early, the case for why laid out like an argument. Thorough. Defensible. The kind of thing that would have earned a second meeting in my old world.

It got nothing. Not bad engagement — no engagement. People scrolled past a well-reasoned case the same way they scroll past an ad, because that's exactly what it read like.

## Where the two games actually diverge

**Who's deciding, and how fast.** In B2B, I was rarely talking to one person. A deal moved through a champion, a budget owner, sometimes a whole committee, each with their own reason to say no. The sales cycle was the relationship. In B2C, one person decides alone, in the time it takes to read a caption. There is no committee. There is a thumb, and it is already moving.

**What counts as proof.** A B2B buyer wants a case study, a reference customer, a number that de-risks the decision internally — because they're not just buying the product, they're defending the choice to someone else later. A B2C buyer isn't defending anything to anyone. They want to see themselves in the story. Proof still matters, but it's proof of relevance, not proof of ROI.

**What the content is actually for.** My old content built a case over a long arc — a whitepaper here, a follow-up email there, each piece adding to an argument that closed months later. B2C content has one job per post: make one person feel understood right now. There is no follow-up email. There is only whether the next three seconds earn the next three.

**What "trust" means.** In B2B, trust was built face to face, deal by deal, over quarters. It doesn't scale, and it isn't supposed to. In B2C, trust has to scale to thousands of strangers who will never get a call with me. That's actually the harder problem, and it's why we went and got the product third-party certified rather than just asserting our own claims — that instinct, that a claim needs external backing to be believed, is the one thing that did transfer cleanly from the B2B side. Everything else about how I built trust had to be rebuilt from zero.

## What I got wrong by not seeing the difference sooner

I kept writing to a buying committee that didn't exist. I was answering objections nobody in a B2C funnel was ever going to raise, while skipping straight past the one question that actually decides a scroll: *does this person understand my problem?*

The fix wasn't a new tactic. It was accepting that the game itself was different, and that being excellent at the old one was actively working against me in the new one.

## The lesson, if you're switching games too

If you're moving from B2B to B2C, or the other way, don't audit your tactics first. Audit your assumption that skill transfers. Some of it does — discipline, the habit of demanding proof before you claim something, treating every promise as something you'll have to answer for. Keep that.

But the buyer is different. The clock is different. What convinces one will bore or alienate the other. I lost real time — and real money — finding that out by trial and error instead of expecting it going in.

Most founders who fail at this don't fail because they're bad at business. They fail because they're good at the wrong game, and it takes them too long to notice they're playing a different one now.

If you've made this switch — which parts of your old playbook survived, and which ones quietly worked against you?
