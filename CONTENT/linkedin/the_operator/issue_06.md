# Energy starts things. Cadence finishes them.

For a long time I built Power Coffee in bursts.

A good week, I'd feel it: ideas everywhere, three projects open, working past midnight because it felt like momentum. Then a bad week would come — a kid sick, a supplier late, a launch that went nowhere — and everything stopped. Not slowed. Stopped. When the energy came back, I'd spend the first days just remembering where I'd left things.

I thought the problem was that I didn't have enough energy. It wasn't. The problem was that energy was the only thing I was running on.

## The people who studied this

In 1911, two teams raced to the South Pole. Roald Amundsen's team set a steady pace — roughly 15 to 20 miles a day — and held it. In bad weather they still marched. In perfect weather they stopped at the target, even when they could have done more. Robert Falcon Scott's team pushed hard on good days and sat in their tents on bad ones. Amundsen got there first and came home. Scott's team reached the Pole weeks later and died on the way back.

Jim Collins and Morten Hansen built a whole idea around this in *Great by Choice*: the **20 Mile March**. The part most people skip is that it cuts both ways. You march on the bad days, and you *stop* on the good ones. Their business example is Southwest Airlines: in 1996, more than a hundred cities were asking for Southwest service. They opened four.

That second half is the hard part for a founder. Stopping when you feel great feels like wasting something.

The same pattern shows up far from polar expeditions:

- **Writing.** The psychologist Robert Boice studied academics who struggled to write. The ones put on a fixed, regular schedule produced several times more pages — and more new ideas — than the ones told to write only when they felt inspired. Waiting for the mood didn't produce the mood.
- **Motivation.** Teresa Amabile and Steven Kramer analyzed about 12,000 daily diary entries from 238 professionals. The single biggest driver of a good workday was *making progress on meaningful work*, and most of that progress was small. Small, regular wins fed the energy — not the other way around.
- **Habits.** In Phillippa Lally's study at UCL, new habits took 66 days on average to become automatic (anywhere from 18 to 254). And missing one day barely moved the needle. What breaks a rhythm isn't a missed day. It's the week you quietly let go.

Put those together and the order flips. We think energy creates output. Mostly, steady output creates the energy.

## What it looks like in a small company

Here's my current version, nothing fancy:

- **Content runs every day at 5am**, whether I'm inspired or not. A script drafts tomorrow, I approve or fix it, it goes out.
- **Outreach runs in small, fixed batches.** I've been inviting people from my own contacts to follow what we're building — a few dozen a day, paced, not 500 on a Sunday night when I'm fired up. Over a couple of weeks that's well over a hundred real, personal invitations. A burst would have done the same number in one night and probably gotten my account flagged.
- **One newsletter a week.** This one. Monday. Even on weeks I don't feel like I have something to say — those often turn out to be the useful ones.

None of these is impressive on a given day. That's the point. A cadence is built so that a bad day still counts.

## The part nobody tells you: cadence can break silently

This weekend I found out our daily content had stopped two days earlier. Nothing dramatic — an account that pays for one of our tools ran out of credit, and the morning job failed quietly. No post on Sunday. Nobody complained, because nobody notices an absence.

That's the real risk of a rhythm. Energy failures are loud: you feel tired, you know you stopped. Cadence failures are silent. The machine just doesn't show up, and the days keep moving.

So a cadence needs two things, not one:

1. **A floor** — the minimum you do no matter what (Amundsen's bad-weather miles).
2. **A tripwire** — something that tells you the same day when the floor wasn't met. Not at the end of the month. The same day.

## Where energy still belongs

I'm not against energy. Energy is great for starting things, for the hard conversation, for the week of a launch. But it's a spike, and you can't plan a company on spikes.

Even my coffee works that way now. One cup, same time every morning, as part of the routine. I stopped chasing a second one at 3pm to rescue a day that had lost its shape. If the afternoon is falling apart, more fuel isn't usually the answer. Going back to the rhythm usually is.

## Try this this week

Pick the one thing that would move your business most if it simply happened every week for the next three months. Then:

- Set the **floor** small enough that you'd hit it on your worst week.
- Set a **ceiling** — the most you'll do even on a great week.
- Set a **tripwire** — how you'll know, same day, that it didn't happen.

Then don't renegotiate it based on how you feel on Tuesday.

### Expansion seeds
- Amundsen vs. Scott: same goal, same ice, different rhythm. One team came home.
- The overlooked half of the 20 Mile March: stopping on the good days. Southwest had 100+ cities asking in 1996 and opened four.
- "Write when inspired" lost to "write on schedule" — in output and in new ideas.
- Progress creates motivation more than motivation creates progress (Amabile & Kramer, ~12,000 diary entries).
- Missing one day barely matters. Letting a week slide does. (Lally, 66 days average.)
- Energy failures are loud. Cadence failures are silent. Build the tripwire.
