# We rebuilt our website in two days. The colors took longer than the code.

The first website I studied for the new thepowercoffee.com sells golf gear.

Not coffee. Not supplements. Not anything in our category. I picked it on purpose, because every functional-coffee site I looked at was making the same moves — dark backgrounds, lightning bolts, a wall of ingredient names, a countdown timer — and I didn't want a better version of that. I wanted a page that felt like a different decision.

So the rebuild started with one question: what does a site look like when it sells one product, to one person, at one moment of their day?

Here's what we analyzed, what we threw out, and why the final palette is navy and cream instead of the black everyone in this category uses.

### 1. Start from somebody else's category

The golf site did three things I wanted to steal:

- **One decision per screen.** Each section asks you one thing. Not "here are 11 benefits," but "here's the problem," then "here's the fix," then "here's the price."
- **Type that does the selling.** Huge, confident headlines. Very little decoration. The words carry the page.
- **Plain-language price.** No "starting at," no mystery bundles.

What we did *not* take: the product photography style, the tone, the layout. Borrow the logic, not the look.

### 2. The palette came from the mascot, not from a mood board

Power Coffee has a mascot: a mug with a lightning bolt, drawn like a vintage screen print. When I looked at it next to our old site, the problem was obvious. The mascot is navy ink on cream paper. The old site was near-black espresso. They looked like two different companies.

So the mascot set the rules:

- **Navy** is the ink. It's the color of the sky at 5:30am, before anyone else is up. It reads calm and trustworthy, which is the opposite of what this category usually signals.
- **Cream** is the paper. Morning light, a kitchen counter, a notebook. Warm, never clinical.
- **Gold** is reserved for action. Buttons, prices, the thing we want you to click. If gold appears and it isn't something you can act on, it's a mistake.

And one rule I now enforce everywhere: **no black backgrounds.** Black is what energy drinks use. It says "intense," "night," "edge." We sell the first win of your morning, and black was quietly telling people we were a different product.

### 3. Fonts set the tone before anyone reads a word

Three typefaces, three jobs:

- **Anton** for headlines: condensed, uppercase, a little athletic, like a jersey number. Confident without shouting.
- **Archivo** for reading: plain and friendly at small sizes.
- **IBM Plex Mono** for labels, prices and lab details. The monospace does real work: it makes "~175mg caffeine per scoop" feel like a measurement, not a slogan.

### 4. I asked for the filter to come off the photos

The first version ran every lifestyle photo through a blue duotone so it matched the palette. It looked cohesive. It also looked cold: people at their desks rendered in blue, like a weather map.

We took the filter off. Original color. A person mid-stride on a sunny street *looks* like energy; a blue-tinted one looks like a mood. Brand consistency matters less than the feeling the photo is supposed to carry.

### 5. Price framed as an add-on, not a discount

The question a coffee drinker actually asks isn't "is $18.90 a good deal?" It's "what does this add to what I already spend?"

So the price section says it directly: one scoop is **$1.26 a morning**, added to the coffee you already buy. No fake "was" prices. A first-order code (FIRST10) and free shipping for newsletter sign-ups, both stated in plain language.

### 6. The hardest part wasn't design. It was the truth audit.

This is the part I didn't expect to take most of the time.

Before launch we traced every number on every page back to the certified lab panel. Not the old marketing doc. The panel. Here's what turned up:

- Our own free recipe guide said **15g** per scoop. The pack, the certified panel and Amazon all say **10g**. We had been publishing the wrong serving size in our own ebook.
- The landing page listed ingredient amounts that no longer matched the certified formula.
- A few stats had drifted in over time that we couldn't source. They're gone.
- Old blog posts quoted customers we couldn't trace back to a real message. We unpublished them. The site now shows four real pieces of customer feedback, and the one from my brother is labeled as my brother.
- We had "verified buyer" and rating language we couldn't back up. It's gone too. In its place: a simple referral offer ($5 for you, $5 for a friend), because a friend's recommendation is proof we can actually stand behind.

None of these were dramatic. They had all just accumulated, the way small errors accumulate on any site that gets edited by many hands over many months. If you haven't traced every number on your site back to its source, I'd bet money something on it is wrong.

### 7. Small things that carry the brand

- The mascot became the browser-tab icon, so even a background tab looks like us.
- The lab certification seal sits near the pack, small enough that it supports the product instead of competing with it.
- A free recipe ebook (10 shakes, one scoop each) gives people a reason to come back that isn't a sale.
- Every change was deployed with a backup and a one-command rollback. A rebuild is only fast if undoing it is fast too.

### How the work actually split

The build was done with an AI (Claude) doing the hands-on work: writing the theme code, rendering every page to check it, running the number-by-number audit, deploying with backups. My job was the calls only I could make: which reference to learn from, that the photos needed their real color back, what to cut when we couldn't prove it, and when it was good enough to ship.

That split is the real lesson for a small team. The AI made the rebuild fast. The judgment about what the brand should feel like, and what we're willing to claim, still had to come from someone who'll answer for it.

### What I'd tell another lean founder

You don't need a bigger budget to rebuild your site. You need three things most teams skip:

1. **A reference from outside your category**, so you're not just remixing your competitors.
2. **A design system derived from something real you already own** (for us, the mascot), so the colors mean something.
3. **A truth audit before anything ships.** Design gets people to read. Accuracy is what makes them trust what they read.

It's too early for conversion numbers. When I have real ones, I'll share them here, good or bad.

— Leo, building Power Coffee one real decision at a time.
