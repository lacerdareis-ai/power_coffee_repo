## SCHEDULED THIS WEEK (5)

### Post 1 — "Same ice, different rhythm" (seed 1) → Nov 10
In 1911, two teams raced to the South Pole.

Amundsen's team marched roughly 15 to 20 miles a day. Bad weather, they still marched. Perfect weather, they stopped at the target anyway.

Scott's team pushed hard on good days and waited in their tents on bad ones.

Same goal. Same ice. Amundsen's team came home. Scott's didn't.

Most founders I know (me included) build like Scott. Big weeks when we feel it, nothing when we don't.

### Post 2 — "Stop on the good days" (seed 2) → Nov 11
The hardest part of a steady pace isn't showing up on bad days.

It's stopping on the good ones.

In 1996, more than 100 cities were asking Southwest Airlines to fly there. They opened four. (Jim Collins tells this story in *Great by Choice*.)

When you feel unstoppable, the move that feels like waste is often the one that keeps you going next year.

**IG treatment:** Quote card, big number "100+ → 4".

### Post 3 — "The mood doesn't come first" (seeds 3 + 4) → Nov 12
"I'll do it when I feel inspired" loses to "I'll do it Tuesday at 7am."

The psychologist Robert Boice studied academics who struggled to write. The ones on a fixed schedule wrote several times more, and had more new ideas, than the ones who waited for inspiration.

Teresa Amabile found the same thing from the other side: after reading about 12,000 work diaries, the biggest boost to people's day was making small progress on work that mattered.

Progress makes the motivation. Not the other way around.

**IG treatment:** Quote card, headline "PROGRESS MAKES THE MOTIVATION."

### Post 4 — "One day vs. one week" (seed 5) → Nov 13
Good news if you missed a day of something you're trying to build: it barely matters.

In Phillippa Lally's habit study at UCL, a new habit took 66 days on average to become automatic, and one missed day hardly changed the result.

What breaks a rhythm isn't the missed day. It's the week you quietly let slide.

So miss a day if you have to. Just don't miss the second one.

**IG treatment:** Quote card, headline "MISS A DAY. NOT TWO."

### Post 5 — "Cadence fails silently" (seed 6) → Nov 14
This week I found out our daily content had stopped two days earlier. An account that one of our tools runs on ran out of credit, and the morning job quietly failed. Nobody complained, because nobody notices something that isn't there.

When your energy runs out, you feel it.
When your cadence stops, nothing tells you.

So every rhythm needs two things: a floor (the minimum you do no matter what) and a tripwire (how you know, the same day, that it didn't happen).

I had the floor. I'm building the tripwire now.

**IG treatment:** Quote card, headline "ENERGY FAILS LOUD. CADENCE FAILS SILENT."

## BANKED (not scheduled)

### VOC post, originally hand-written for Nov 10, moved here 2026-11-09
I had someone read 1,589 comments from people talking about the afternoon slump. Not about our product. About their lives.

The biggest surprise: almost nobody blames their coffee. They blame lunch. "Carb coma" came up again and again, and their first fixes were free ones: a walk, a glass of water, a lighter plate.

The second surprise: people search for the problem, not the category. "Coffee crash" gets about nine times the searches of "coffee booster", the word we'd been leading with.

So we're changing how we talk. Less about what's in the scoop, more about the moment people already know: the post-lunch slump, and the 3pm cup they'd rather skip.

If you sell something, go read what your customers write when they're not talking to you. It's humbling, and it's cheap.
