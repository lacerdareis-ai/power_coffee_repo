# I fired someone at 4:47pm on a Thursday. The time was the mistake.

I didn't choose 4:47pm on purpose. That was just when the conversation
finally happened — I'd been putting it off all day, and it caught up with
me late.

I've since learned that late afternoon is close to the worst possible
window for a conversation like that. Not because of willpower. Because of
a receptor.

Here's the mechanism, and it's not complicated: your brain runs on a
molecule called adenosine that builds up the longer you've been awake —
it's the actual chemical signal behind feeling tired, foggy, short-fused.
Caffeine doesn't remove that buildup. It blocks the receptor that reads
it, for roughly as long as the caffeine stays in your system — about five
hours, on average.

So if your last real coffee was late morning, the block is wearing off
right around late afternoon. The adenosine was never gone. It was just
muted. By 4:47pm, it's back, in full, exactly when a hard conversation
needs you at your most controlled — not your most depleted.

I'd scheduled the hardest decision of that week for the worst possible
hour of my own day, and I didn't know it, because nobody schedules around
a receptor. We schedule around calendars.

This isn't a caffeine pitch. It's an operator pitch: **the thing you're
protecting when you manage your energy isn't your energy. It's the
quality of the decisions you make with it.** A founder who's sharp at
10am and running on fumes at 4pm isn't one person all day — they're two
different decision-makers, and most of us let the calendar pick which one
handles the hard stuff.

I changed one thing after that Thursday: anything consequential — a hard
conversation, a pricing call, a hire or a fire — gets scheduled against my
own curve now, not just against open calendar slots. Sometimes that means
earlier. Sometimes it means a second, smaller dose of something steady
later in the day instead of pushing through on willpower alone. The
mechanism doesn't care which — it just means knowing where you actually
are on it before you schedule something that matters.

Most performance advice tells you to manage your time. The actual lever
is managing *when* on your own curve the important decisions land.
